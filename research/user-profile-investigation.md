# Comp 6 — User Profile Investigation

**Source:** Phone call with Dad, a practising clinical physiotherapist. A single-source investigation grounding the user framing in domain-expert tacit knowledge — how a real clinician works, weighs sources, and teaches — rather than a secondhand approximation. The desk-research synthesis lives in `competitive-landscape.md`.

---

## Section A — Sub-specialty

**What phase of stroke do you see most — acute, subacute, or chronic?**
Chronic.

**What body system / function dominates your caseload?**
Lower limb and gait.

**What setting do you work in?**
Community.

---

## Section B — When would he actually use a research tool?

**When you're prepping for a complex new patient, what do you want to know that isn't readily available?**

Usually they can't describe their previous treatment very well themselves. I ask them. I look at the homework that they've been given, which is still usually two sheets of A4 with stick-men diagrams and some instructions on how to exercise. The last person I asked, "How are you getting on with those?" — they couldn't even read the writing. It was typewritten, but they couldn't read it.

If I had time to be completely prepared, what I'd need is results of tests. That ordinarily means an MRI report, written by a consultant radiologist who's read the MRI or CAT scans — if you get a good one. I usually don't have access to that. Those reports are created inside the NHS, and they're extremely cautious about sharing information, even with people inside the NHS. Some of them are downright obstructive.

Do I look for research on conditions — papers, Google, YouTube — in my clinical work? Not usually.

**When a junior PT brings you a treatment plan to review, what do you check?**

A junior coming to me with a formal written treatment plan to sign off — that's never really happened. I've been to lots of joint sessions with junior physios where they'll show me how they're getting on. I can see what the results are so far, they give me an idea of what they've been working on, and I'll usually suggest expanding that into such-and-such an area, which would be more targeted in its effect.

In the NHS — pretty much the only place this happens — they come to you if they feel stuck. Sometimes you have the luxury of saying, "Right, I've got an hour spare on Monday at 11:00, let's do a joint treatment." They don't usually come with their own proposed plan looking for advice; they'll say "Can you come and help me with this," and we go into a treatment room and work at it for a bit. Then: "Have you got any better ideas?"

**Have you ever changed a clinical practice based on new evidence? What triggered it?**

Yes. Invariably it's presentations, lectures, and formal training courses. In practice I don't do an awful lot of looking outside of those times, because the clinical work is always so demanding. Until you set aside the time — "I'm going to this training course, I'm going to this lecture" — that's the only time you really get to stop and think about how you approach your problems in any new way based on research.

Mostly I work from the now extremely wide variety of experience and different treatment approaches. I will cheerfully switch from what you might call a Bobath approach to a PNF approach to a Brunnstrom approach to a Johnstone approach to a Peto approach. These are all different ways that have been developed. Carr and Shepherd, and CIMT, are two more I'd left off that list. You just pick from the menu you already know — you don't write a new menu until you go on a course.

Where this would really help: the senior on a stroke ward typically has a small team of six to nine — a couple of middle seniors, a few juniors, some assistants. If you had a research assistant that could pull all the most modern information on treatment and clinical practice, and synthesise it into a presentation the senior could give the team monthly, everyone could stay up to date super quickly — no paid training courses, no weekends away. One meeting a month where the whole team is brought up to date. If you did that for in-house training, that'd be awesome.

The far bigger thing behind all of this is the electronic health record. Britain doesn't really have one — what it has is diabolical. The new version, Medius, is being introduced this year, but only for GP practices; to my knowledge it doesn't yet include hospital care. This is the problem-oriented (or patient-oriented) medical record, designed by Dr. Lawrence Weed — he started on it perhaps as early as the 1950s, it was in full swing by the 1960s, and it was fully electronic on touch-screen computers at the University of Vermont by the mid-60s. With a proper EHR you could take any problem and almost instantly search the entire database — in this country alone that's around 70 million living patient records, and perhaps 100 million if you include those who've died in the intervening decades. Imagine the volume of research information you could extract at the touch of a button. The access mechanism is a permission: the relevant authority — either the patient, or whoever holds their health power of attorney — requests from the system admin a renewal permission allowing specific individuals into someone's full health record, and indeed to contribute to it.

## Section C — Source credibility

**Which sources do you trust most for stroke-rehab evidence?**

You've got various databases: PubMed, CINAHL — those are the main ones you'd go to. Cochrane is the other one.

**What sources do you actively distrust or discount?**

Honestly there aren't any I really distrust. Any source of study, you can criticise the model that produced the information: you can criticise single case studies as not being statistically significant; you can criticise statistic-based studies for a lack of individuality. If a conference uploaded online only contained an abstract and didn't go through to a full paper, I'd generally trust that that sort of activity has been conducted by serious professionals doing their best and being pretty objective — as much as you can be in a science that is at least half art and half science, because medicine is about people.

**When two good sources disagree, what do you do?**

Debate. Debate into the evidence that they're basing their opinions on. To give an example: there is a well-known narrative that the Bobath approach doesn't really work, and that there's evidence to show it doesn't. What the evidence actually showed is that Bobath isn't any *better* than other approaches — it doesn't say it isn't good — but that narrative has been added to and added to by people. (Becoming a Bobath tutor is one of the things I tried to become myself, around 2003 or so.)

## Section D — Investigative vs. summarising

**When a junior PT asks you a clinical question, do you answer immediately? If not, what do you ask back first?**

It depends how time-critical it is that they get their answer. If it's more about practice and I'm trying to teach them, generally you want them to lay out what they already know about it. Then you can identify blocks in their thinking — knowledge they already possess but aren't using, a link between two ideas they haven't made.

Here's a great example. When everybody goes to physio college, they're taught about movement happening in a sagittal plane or a coronal plane — but they're only ever taught about *balance* in the sagittal and coronal planes. They're not taught about balance in the transverse or horizontal plane, so they haven't made that connection.

My first questions back are: what do you think the problem is, and what methods have you tried so far?

**If a research partner handed you "10 papers on stroke rehab", what would you find useless about that?**

Most of it. Research papers are incredibly wordy and long-winded, and involve statistical analysis I've never been trained in. The purpose of most research is twofold: to find out new or interesting stuff, and to give the researcher a job — preferably a nice full-time one in a university. Which is why abstracts and summaries are generally pretty on it: a ten-page paper is summarised in one or two paragraphs, and that will probably tell you the bulk of what you really need to know to apply it.

**What's a clinical decision where the evidence was thin or conflicting? How did you handle the uncertainty?**

The evidence is conflicting on facilitation. The evidence says that facilitation of movement is not a thing — that it's a construct in the minds of the proponents of Bobath theory, a concept within the neurological approach to neurological treatment. The evidence says facilitation doesn't work; clinical experience says otherwise.

## Post-call synthesis + pivot

**Critical finding — pivot the user.** This call revealed that Dad-as-practitioner doesn't reach for research in his own clinical work: he re-refers rather than searches, doesn't usually look things up, changes practice mainly off the back of external training courses, and prefers abstracts to full papers. Designing the AI researcher for *his* day-to-day would produce a tool he wouldn't use — a summariser at best. The brief explicitly asks for investigator-not-summariser behaviour.

**User pivot landed on:** a UK-based CPD course tutor delivering weekend courses on stroke rehabilitation to practising physiotherapists. This surfaced from his description of the receiving-side benefit of monthly evidence-update sessions for NHS ward teams. After sparring on user viability (in-house ward team-lead → external contracting educator → academic researcher → CPD course provider) we landed on the CPD course tutor — a real paid role, with a real workflow and a real differentiator against likely competition.

**What this call still gives us (transferable to the pivoted user):**

- **Therapeutic schools landscape:** Bobath / PNF / Brunnstrom / Johnstone / Peto / Carr-Shepherd / CIMT. A real stroke-rehab CPD tutor must navigate these as *traditions* — with founders, evidence bases, contemporary defenders and critics — not just as papers. **This is the build's primary differentiator.**
- **The Bobath debate:** advocacy-position vs. honest reading of evidence. The AI must separate *"X isn't better than Y"* from *"X doesn't work"*, and surface vested interests in advocate positions.
- **Teaching mode:** *"What do you think the problem is? What have you tried?"* — a direct template for the AI's prior-position-elicitation pattern.
- **Facilitation conflict:** evidence says one thing, clinical experience says another. The AI must hold space for clinician pushback against the literature.
- **Source tiers:** PubMed, CINAHL, Cochrane named as primary; abstract-density preferred over full-paper sprawl; no strong distrust of any source class, but a readiness to critique the methods of any study.

**Locked design features (from pivot sparring):**

- **Clinical-safety / epistemic stance.** The AI offers pattern-matched, sourced material; the tutor verifies primary literature; the AI never speaks authoritatively about clinical effectiveness.
- **UK geographic anchor.** NICE / RCP / Stroke Association = primary guidelines. AHA / Australian Stroke Foundation = secondary.
- **Domain boundary.** In scope: physio CPD content (motor recovery, gait, balance, upper limb, outcome measures, swallowing/cognition overlap, discharge planning). Out of scope: pharmacology, surgery, imaging interpretation, acute medical stabilisation.
- **Debate-vs-lookup routing.** Interpretive / contested questions → debate mode; factual / settled-evidence questions → lookup mode. Driven by *"how time-critical it is that they get their answer."*
- **Prior-position elicitation.** The AI asks the tutor's working position before a debate begins.
- **Team discovery.** The AI asks the tutor 4-6 questions about the team they're about to brief (caseload, prior CPD, weak spots, the booker's stated goal).
- **No slide production.** The AI's output ends at structured brief inputs; the tutor builds the deck.

**Tone:** accessible but credible. Source citations as `Author Year + strength tag` (e.g. *"Cochrane 2018 — high-quality SR"*), not full academic format. Practical-clinical depth rather than methodological epistemology.

**Sub-specialty:** stroke rehab, broad (tutors cover the breadth their teams need). A demo example can narrow to one topic for vividness without narrowing the AI's scope.

**The one phrase that stuck:** *"The evidence actually showed Bobath isn't better than other approaches, not that it doesn't work — but that narrative has been added to and added to by people."* The canonical example for the build's claim-vs-evidence-base behaviour and its surfacing-vested-interests pattern.
