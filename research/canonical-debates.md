## Debate 1 — Bobath as treatment method vs Bobath as movement-observation framework

### The contested claim

*"Bobath should be defended as a treatment intervention for stroke"* — vs — *"Bobath should be defended only as a clinical-reasoning / movement-observation framework, distinct from its evidence base as a treatment method."*

This is the framework-vs-method distinction [schools-landscape § fault line 2] surfaced via the Penny quote, returned here with the canonical source citations.

### "For" position — defenders of Bobath as a continuing, valid practice

**Named contemporary defenders (UK + international institutional):**
- **Margaret Mayston** (Senior Teaching Fellow, UCL Faculty of Life Sciences; Senior Bobath Tutor, worked with the Bobaths personally) [1]. Mayston's 2008 *Physiotherapy Research International* editorial "Bobath Concept: Bobath@50: mid-life crisis — What of the future?" is the canonical nuanced-defender source.
- **Gill Stern** (past President, European Bobath Tutors' Association / EBTA), with the EBTA Executive Committee [2]. Author of the formal EBTA response to Novak et al. 2013.
- **Bobath Tutors UK / EBTA / IBITA** (International Bobath Instructors Training Association) — institutional defenders maintaining course delivery.
- **Nikki Penny** (UK neuro physio, ex-Bobath tutor track, declared no financial stake) [competitive-landscape § P2 Friction B].

**Evidence Mayston cites for the framework position [1]:**
- The Bobath Concept "is currently taught and practised in some shape or form, by thousands of clinicians in many countries throughout the world" — empirical persistence as evidence of clinical utility.
- The Bobaths' own 1975/1990 statements that "other methods and ways of treatment ... should be explored" and "other techniques described by other workers (Kabat and Knott, 1954; Voss, 1967) may also need to be used at certain stages of treatment" — i.e., the original founders did not claim Bobath was a complete package.
- Tyson & Selley 2007 finding: UK therapists surveyed "seemed to follow a traditional Bobath model in practice, despite their self-perception of being eclectic" — establishing the framework's deep penetration into how UK neuro physios think, even when they describe their practice in non-Bobath terms.
- Davidson & Waters 2000: 88% of UK neurological physiotherapists used the Bobath approach.

**Mayston's actual position (load-bearing quote for the build) [1]:** *"There is an urgent need for an integrated approach to neurorehabilitation that is not based on approaches, but rather is client based with a sound theoretical, and where possible, evidence base. This does not negate the practice and teaching of Bobath-based therapy, but requires a shift in focus to recognize Bobath as a contributor to client-based neurorehabilitation, not the leading actor who wishes to be centre stage at all times."*

Mayston's recommended steps for the Bobath Concept's future [1]:
1. Define the Bobath Concept as Bobath described it.
2. Determine what is still relevant and discard what is not.
3. Provide evidence and theoretical basis based on current literature.
4. Accept that Bobath alone does not provide a complete package — other approaches and adjuncts are complementary or even preferable for some clients.

**EBTA / Novak 2013 rebuttal [2]:** the EBTA's 2013 response to the Novak et al. *Dev Med Child Neurol* SR (which gave NDT/Bobath a "red light, don't do it" verdict for cerebral palsy) defends Bobath as a *clinical-reasoning tool*: "Bobath analysis as a tool, can be used with every type of neuro-disability. It involves 'observation, analysis, interpretation, experimentation, outcome measurement' (Figure 1, Mayston 2008)." Frames Bobath therapy's heterogeneity as a methodological challenge for SRs (different therapists deliver different sessions to different clients), not as Bobath therapy failing.

**UK anchoring:** Mayston @ UCL; Bobath Centre London (50th anniversary 2007, still operates); Bobath Tutors UK / EBTA institutionally; Davidson & Waters 2000 UK survey; Tyson & Selley 2007 UK survey.

### "Against" position — critics of Bobath as a treatment method

**Named contemporary critics (UK + international):**
- **Sarah Tyson** (UK neuro physio educator/researcher) — quote on vested interests + "cults rather than applied science" [competitive-landscape § P2 Friction B].
- **Zack Siddells** (former UK neuro physio teacher) — "more-or-less complete failure of trials investigating BB to show benefit ... continue to flog a dead horse" [competitive-landscape § P2 Friction B].
- **JB8205** (UK acute neuroscience unit physio) [competitive-landscape § P2 Friction B].
- **Ruud van der Veen** (Netherlands physio, "Bobath was abandoned in the Netherlands in 2007") [competitive-landscape § P2 Friction B].
- **Erik Prinsen** ("evidence-based facts outweigh empirical evidence") [competitive-landscape § P2 Friction B].
- **Iona Novak** et al. 2013 (cerebral palsy "red light, don't do it" verdict prompting EBTA's rebuttal) [2].

**Systematic-review evidence the critics cite:**
- **Kollen et al. 2009** (*Stroke*, AHA Journals) [10]: SR of 16 RCTs. Best-evidence synthesis. Conclusion (verbatim): *"This systematic review confirms that overall the Bobath Concept is not superior to other approaches. Based on best evidence synthesis, no evidence is available for the superiority of any approach."* Specific trials cited within:
  - Platz et al. (Arm BASIS training, n=42, 4 weeks): "Exercise therapy time based on arm BASIS training, proved to be superior to augmented exercise therapy time based on Bobath therapy" on Fugl-Meyer arm motor score [10].
  - Thaut et al. (Rhythmic-auditory stimulation [RAS] vs NDT/Bobath gait training, n=78): "gains were significantly higher for RAS compared with NDT/Bobath training" on gait velocity, stride length, cadence, swing symmetry [10].
  - Langhammer & Stanghelle (MRP vs Bobath, n=48): "MRP is superior to, but not statistically significant different from, Bobath" in acute rehabilitation phase [10].
  - Wang et al. (orthopedic approach vs Bobath, n=44): "Patients benefit more from the Bobath treatment in MAS and SIS scores than from the orthopedic treatment program" [10] — i.e., one trial *favours* Bobath against orthopedic.
- **Pathak et al. 2021** (*J Family Med Prim Care*, PROSPERO CRD42019125400) [3]: Bobath SR. Verdict: *"No strong documentation describing the effectiveness of this neuro-developmental treatment or its supporting neuro-developmental treatment in comparison with other advanced neuro-physiotherapeutic techniques has been found ... This study is inconclusive in determining the effectiveness of the Bobath approach."*
- **Scrivener et al. 2020** + **Pollock et al. 2014** — both find Bobath inferior to task-specific training, not superior to combined interventions or strength training.

### Where the genuine evidence sits

- Multiple SRs across 16 years (Kollen 2009 [10], Pollock 2014, Scrivener 2020, Pathak 2021 [3]) converge: Bobath is **not superior** to other approaches on the trials directly comparing it. Best-evidence-synthesis verdict: **"no evidence available for superiority of any approach"** [10].
- Bobath is **inferior** to task-specific training for lower-limb activities in meta-analyses (Pollock 2014, Scrivener 2020).
- **No SR has shown Bobath inferior on every outcome to every comparator** — there are trials where Bobath outperforms specific comparators (orthopedic approach [10]; passive control conditions; PNF for standing balance [schools-landscape § PNF]).
- **No SR has tested the framework-vs-treatment distinction Mayston [1] proposes.** The literature treats Bobath as an intervention condition in trials; the framework claim is theoretical, not empirical. This is a load-bearing observation: the "Bobath as framework" defence is *not refutable by intervention trials* because intervention trials don't test framework claims.

### Vested-interest signal

- **Mayston is a Senior Bobath Tutor** [1] — declared affiliation in the editorial itself; this is institutional vested interest. She acknowledges it implicitly by framing her editorial as critically reviewing the concept she has tutored on.
- **EBTA** as authors of the Novak rebuttal [2] — institutional defender body whose members have commercial course-delivery interests.
- **Penny declares no financial stake** [competitive-landscape § P2 Friction B] — explicit declaration as a "neutral" defender of the framework position.
- **Vested interest cuts both ways**: defenders hold commercial course-delivery stakes; critics can hold their own non-commercial stakes (reputation, career investment in the competing evidence-based paradigm). Surface both directions.

### UK-specific anchoring

- **UCL** (Mayston) [1]; **Bobath Centre London** [1]; **Bobath Tutors UK / EBTA** [2]; **Tyson & Selley 2007 UK survey** [1]; **Davidson & Waters 2000 UK survey** [1]; **Lennon et al. 2001 UK survey** [1]; **Sarah Tyson UK blog** [competitive-landscape § P2 Friction B]; **JB8205 UK acute neuroscience unit** [competitive-landscape § P2 Friction B].

### Named gaps

- **No direct empirical test of the framework-vs-treatment distinction.** The "Bobath as observation framework" defence is essentially un-falsifiable within current SR methodology because SRs test interventions, not frameworks.
- **EBTA's quantitative scale of UK course delivery 2024-2026** not retrieved; still an open gap.
- **Mayston's most recent (post-2020) position not retrieved** — 2008 editorial is the canonical source but Mayston has continued to publish; whether her 2020s position has shifted is not in scope here.

## Debate 2 — Facilitation: real mechanism or therapist-construct?

### The contested claim

*"Facilitation (manual handling by the therapist to elicit a more normal movement response) is a real neurophysiological mechanism — therapist hands change the patient's motor output via measurable neural processes"* — vs — *"Facilitation is a construct in the mind of Bobath proponents — the changes attributed to facilitation are explained by biomechanical / muscle-length / motor-learning / placebo / observer-bias processes; no specific neurophysiological mechanism is established."*

Dad's call Q14 [user-profile-investigation.md] explicitly named this debate: *"the evidence says facilitation is not a thing, that it's a construct in the mind of the proponents of the Bobath theory."*

### "For" position — facilitation as a real mechanism

**Named defenders:**
- **Berta Bobath** (1970, 1978, 1990 textbooks, cited in [1]) — introduced "inhibition" and "facilitation" as physiological explanations for the effects of handling.
- **EBTA / Bobath Tutors UK** [2] — institutional position: handling reduces hypertonia, and "Uniquely developed specialised handling to reduce hypertonia is used where appropriate, but this is not 'passive', and to describe Bobath as 'passive handling' Table 1 (Novak et al) is inaccurate."

**Evidence "for" position cites:**
- Brunnstrom's seven-stage recovery framework + reflex-hierarchical motor-control theory (Sherrington reflex theory, hierarchical CNS organisation — see Levin & Piscitelli 2022 [4] for the lineage). These provided the theoretical basis for facilitation as a mid-20th-century concept.
- Clinical-experience reports of immediate change in patient movement quality after therapist handling — Penny's quote on Bobath courses as "movement courses that allowed me to begin to understand movement ... see an experienced clinician identify movement problems and begin to develop a treatment plan forward" [competitive-landscape § P2 Friction B].

### "Against" position — facilitation as a construct

**Named critics:**
- **Mayston 2008 herself** [1] — explicitly retracts the "inhibition" claim as a physiological explanation: *"tone, which the Bobaths emphasized as an essential component of functional activity, is now known to comprise both neural and non-neural components ... the word 'inhibition' is no longer a relevant explanation for the ways Bobath therapists stretch and activate muscles."* And later: *"suggesting inhibition is a physiological explanation for what therapists achieve by handling is not scientifically correct. Therapists are effecting changes in both inhibitory and excitatory synapses simultaneously, but their 'inhibition' also affects visco-elastic properties of muscle and by improving muscle length can gain a better biomechanical advantage for more efficient muscle action."* This is a **defender retracting the original mechanism claim** — i.e., even the leading UK Bobath tutor concedes facilitation/inhibition is not what the Bobaths claimed it was, mechanistically.
- **Levin & Piscitelli 2022** (*Motor Control* journal) [4]: critique of the underlying motor-control theory facilitation rests on. Argues that Sherrington's reflex theory and Foerster's hierarchical CNS organisation have been "misused to describe how the nervous system controls movements while they actually describe different processes by which control leads to the execution of movement, without addressing what is actually controlled." Frames the broader problem: **there is no consensus motor-control theory underlying neurorehabilitation practice**; the field uses "a hodgepodge of different models, hypotheses, principles, and theories alternatively describing features of the control and execution of movement." Facilitation-based approaches rest on the "direct framework" (Wolpert & Ghahramani 2000 internal-models; Todorov & Jordan 2002 optimal feedback control) — Levin & Piscitelli argue this framework has "several limitations ... the fact that neurons have threshold properties is overlooked. Notably, computational theories are physiologically incompatible with the nonlinear properties of motoneurons since motoneuronal input/output functions are irreversible (Feldman, 2019)." Implication for facilitation: the neurophysiological model underlying mid-20th-century facilitation claims is itself contested at the foundational level.
- **Pollock 2014, Scrivener 2020, Pathak 2021** [3], **Kollen 2009** [10] — all SRs of Bobath as intervention. None demonstrate Bobath/facilitation outperforms task-specific training on motor recovery outcomes. **The intervention-level evidence does not support facilitation as a causally-load-bearing therapeutic mechanism** above and beyond practice intensity, task-specificity, and attention.

**Critics' framing of what's actually happening when "facilitation" appears to work:**
- **Biomechanical / muscle-length:** stretch via handling changes visco-elastic muscle properties [1, Mayston quote above].
- **Motor learning:** practice with verbal/visual/manual prompting drives motor relearning, with the manual prompts being one of many possible cues, not specifically causal [schools-landscape § Carr-Shepherd MRP].
- **Therapist attention / non-specific effects:** the hands-on attention provides feedback the patient uses, but the *mechanism* is feedback-driven motor learning, not facilitation-of-normal-movement-pattern.
- **Observer bias / clinical-anchoring effects:** the therapist's expectations and the patient's response to interpersonal interaction can produce apparent change without a specific neurophysiological mechanism.

### Where the genuine evidence sits

- **No SR or meta-analysis has demonstrated facilitation-as-mechanism** as causally separable from practice intensity, task-specificity, attention, and biomechanical effects.
- **The most authoritative defender (Mayston [1]) has retracted the original mechanism claim** while defending continued Bobath practice on framework / clinical-utility grounds.
- **The broader motor-control theoretical landscape (Levin & Piscitelli 2022 [4])** does not support a unified neurophysiological model on which facilitation could rest. Two competing frameworks (direct / indirect) exist; neither maps cleanly onto facilitation claims.
- **What remains** is the empirical observation (e.g., Penny's [competitive-landscape § P2 Friction B]) that Bobath-trained therapists report clinically-useful movement-observation skills — which is the *framework* claim of Debate 1, not the *mechanism* claim of Debate 2.

### Vested-interest signal

- See Debate 1 — the institutional vested interest applies to facilitation specifically because facilitation is a load-bearing course-content item for Bobath / NDT credentialing.
- Levin & Piscitelli are McGill researchers (motor-control academics) with academic-prestige incentive but no commercial stake in any specific therapeutic school.

### UK-specific anchoring

- **Mayston @ UCL** [1] — load-bearing UK source acknowledging the mechanism claim is retracted.
- **Dad's call** (`user-profile-investigation.md` § D) — UK clinical voice naming the conflict explicitly.

### Named gaps

- **Direct comparative studies of facilitation vs no-facilitation in matched practice conditions** not retrieved within web-cap. Would require a different search strategy targeting specific RCT designs that hold practice constant and vary only therapist hands-on facilitation.
- **Qualitative research on therapist beliefs about facilitation** (e.g. Lennon et al. 2001, Lennon 2003 type studies) not deeply retrieved — could sharpen the "construct in the therapist's mind" claim with primary qualitative evidence.
- **Pellecchia 2017 / similar specific facilitation-mechanism critiques** not retrieved — would need targeted search.

## Debate 3 — CIMT in NHS-resourced settings (standard vs mCIMT)

### The contested claim

*"Constraint-induced movement therapy (CIMT) should be a standard NHS upper-limb intervention for eligible chronic stroke patients despite its high-intensity protocol (6 hr/day × 2 weeks)"* — vs — *"Standard CIMT is impractical for routine NHS delivery; modified CIMT (mCIMT) protocols are acceptable alternatives, but their evidence base is different and the trade-offs matter clinically."*

### "For" position — CIMT and mCIMT are robust, NHS should deliver

**Named defenders:**
- **Edward Taub** (originator, UAB) + **Wolf et al.** (EXCITE trial, JAMA 2006).
- **European Stroke Organisation 2023 guidelines** [via Olaoye et al. 2024, ref 9]: **"Strongly recommends traditional or modified CIMT"** — strongest of the three guideline bodies. ESO does not impose qualifying motor-function criteria.

**Evidence for [5] (Kwakkel + Veerbeek 2015 SR/meta-analysis, n=1784 patients, 51 RCTs):**
- **Original CIMT (EXCITE trial only [Wolf 2006]):** significant positive medium effect sizes post intervention for arm-hand activities, self-reported amount of arm-hand use, self-reported quality of arm-hand movement, sustained at 4-month follow-up. Long-term significant positive effects on QoL related to hand function and ADL [5].
- **mCIMT (44 RCTs, n=1397):** significant positive small-to-medium summary effect sizes (Hedges' g) post intervention for motor function of the paretic arm, muscle tone, arm-hand activities, self-reported amount + quality of arm-hand use in daily life, basic ADL. Effects sustained at mean 21.6-week follow-up for motor function, arm-hand activities, self-reported amount + quality of arm-hand use (NOT for muscle tone or basic ADL) [5].
- **Forced Use alone** (mitt only, no shaping): **no added value** — confirms that shaping + repetitive exercises + behavioural-change instructions are the load-bearing components, not the constraint alone [5].
- **Sensitivity analysis** [5]: no significant differences between original CIMT and mCIMT, between dose levels (5-60 hours, mean 47), or between timing (<3 months vs ≥3 months post-stroke) **with one important exception:** mCIMT effects on motor function (FMA-arm) restricted to trials started within first 3 months post-stroke. This is the meaningful timing-of-delivery finding.
- **Mechanism finding:** "improvements introduced by original CIMT or mCIMT are mainly based on adaptation by learning to optimize the use of intact end-effectors by selecting patients with some voluntary motor control of wrist and finger extensors post stroke" [5]. I.e., CIMT works largely by **compensation strategy learning**, not neural repair, except possibly in the first 3 months.

**Eligibility criteria (load-bearing for NHS-resource debate) [5]:**
- Voluntary extension ≥20° wrist + ≥10° finger extension (higher-functioning) OR ≥10° wrist + ≥10° thumb + ≥10° two-finger extension (lower-functioning).
- MMSE ≥24, no major medical contraindications, able to stand ≥2 minutes, no excessive pain/spasticity.
- **About 10% (range 3-90%) of initially screened patients were eligible across included trials.** [5]

### "Against" position — practical NHS-resource critique + nuance on what works

**Named critics:**
- **NICE NG236 2023** [6, 7] (Recommendation 1.13.19): **"Consider** constraint-induced movement therapy for people after stroke who have movement of 20 degrees of wrist extension and 10 degrees of finger extension. Be aware of potential adverse events (such as falls, low mood and fatigue)." Note: "Consider" is the weakest NICE strength term (below "Offer" / "Recommend"). NICE positions CIMT as cautious, eligibility-restricted, and with adverse-event flags.
- **Intercollegiate Stroke Working Party (ISWP) / RCP 2023** [8, via 9]: Same "Consider" language, same eligibility criteria. Note: RCP separately recommends task-specific / repetitive task training (RTT) more strongly as the "principal rehabilitation method alongside traditional exercise."
- **Implementation-feasibility critics** — implied across the literature: 6 hr/day × 2 weeks is a service-delivery model NHS outpatient stroke services rarely deliver. mCIMT is a workable alternative but the dose-response evidence is mixed.

**Evidence the critics cite:**
- **Kwakkel & Veerbeek 2015 itself** [5]: "About 10% of initially screened patients were eligible for (m)CIMT" — i.e., **CIMT serves a narrow eligibility subset of stroke survivors**, which constrains its NHS service-design role.
- **Mechanism finding** [5]: "compensatory skill learning rather than 'true neurological repair' or 'recovery' of impairments" — this re-frames CIMT's clinical claim more conservatively than original Taub mechanism narrative ("learned non-use" reversal).
- **NICE / RCP adverse-event flag** [6, 8]: falls, low mood, fatigue. Practical NHS-delivery implication: requires monitoring beyond the CIMT session itself.
- **Suputtitada et al. trial within Kollen 2009 SR** [10]: CIMT vs Bobath in chronic stroke (n=69) — **"No statistically significant differences were observed between CIMT and Bobath for ARAT, hand grip, and pinch strength."** I.e., CIMT is not categorically superior to other approaches in every direct comparison.

### Where the genuine evidence sits

- **Strong evidence (Kwakkel & Veerbeek 2015 meta-analysis [5]):** CIMT and mCIMT both produce clinically meaningful improvements on arm-hand activities + self-reported amount/quality of use in daily life. Effects sustained 4-5 months. The dose-and-protocol differences between original and modified CIMT **do not produce significantly different outcomes** in pooled meta-analysis.
- **The 3-month window matters:** mCIMT's motor-function (FMA-arm) effect is restricted to <3-month-post-stroke initiation [5]. After this window, the gains are compensation-based, not impairment-recovery-based.
- **Forced Use without shaping doesn't work** [5] — the active ingredient is repetitive task practice + behavioural shaping, not the constraint alone.
- **UK guideline position (NICE NG236 [6,7] + ISWP/RCP 2023 [8]):** Consider, with eligibility criteria + adverse-event flags. ESO 2023 [9]: Strongly recommend. The transatlantic / cross-jurisdictional disagreement IS the debate.

### Vested-interest signal

- **UAB Taub-lab licensing** of CIMT-Plus training programmes — moderate institutional commercial stake.
- **Less politically loaded** than Bobath in UK NHS context — no named tutor-association equivalent to EBTA / Bobath Tutors UK.

### UK-specific anchoring

- **NICE NG236 1.13.19** (England + Wales) [6, 7].
- **ISWP / RCP National Clinical Guideline for Stroke 2023** [8] — UK + Ireland.
- **Olaoye et al. 2024 Healthcare 12:1433** [9] — explicit UK-vs-ESO guideline comparison from a UK-affiliated review.

### Named gaps

- **mCIMT-specific subgroup data on NHS-delivery feasibility (cost, staffing requirements per patient)** not retrieved. The 6-hr/day vs lower-intensity-longer-duration trade-off has an economic dimension this pass doesn't unpack.
- **UK-specific CIMT delivery patterns 2024-2026** (which Trusts deliver, what protocols) not retrieved.
- **Direct comparison of mCIMT vs task-specific training within first 3 months** — both intervention types recommended by NICE / RCP, but their direct head-to-head evidence not separately surfaced.

## Debate 4 — Brunnstrom stages framework vs Brunnstrom therapy

### The contested claim

*"Brunnstrom therapy (using synergies and reflexes to facilitate voluntary motor control) is a viable contemporary stroke rehabilitation intervention"* — vs — *"Brunnstrom therapy as an intervention is largely superseded; only the Brunnstrom Stages framework (as a staging tool) retains contemporary utility."*

This is a different shape from Debates 1-3 — both positions can be partly true.

### "For" position — Brunnstrom therapy as a contemporary intervention

**Named defender (direct RCT evidence):**
- **Pandian, Arya & Davidson 2011** (*Journal of Bodywork and Movement Therapies* 16(3): 330-337, PMID 22703742) [11] — the previously-flagged "Wang 2011" comparison study is actually Pandian-Arya-Davidson (Indian institute affiliated with University of Delhi). RCT, n=30 chronic post-stroke (35.06 ± 14.52 months). Group A: Brunnstrom Hand Manipulation (BHM) — "the hand treatment protocol of the Brunnstrom movement therapy, which uses synergies and reflexes to develop voluntary motor control." Group B: Motor Relearning Programme (MRP) hand protocol — "the practice of specific motor skills." Outcome measures: Brunnstrom Recovery Stages of Hand (BRS-H), Fugl-Meyer Assessment Wrist + Hand (FMA-WH).

**Pandian 2011 results [11]:**
- Both protocols effective (BRS-H: p=0.003 to 0.004; FMA-WH: p<0.001).
- **Statistically significant in favour of Group A (BHM) for FMA-WH (p<0.004) and FMA item VIII (hand motor recovery, p<0.033).**
- Conclusion: *"BHM was found to be more effective than MRP in rehabilitation of the hand in chronic post-stroke patients."*

This is a **non-trivial finding for the build's debate-mode behaviour** because it directly contradicts the broader SR-level message ("task-specific training beats facilitation-based approaches") for the specific case of chronic-phase hand recovery in n=30 from a non-Western context.

**Limitations on the "for" claim:**
- Small sample (n=30, 15 per arm).
- Single-centre Indian rehabilitation institute — generalisability to UK NHS not established.
- Outcome measure BRS-H is derived from the Brunnstrom framework itself — potential measurement bias toward detecting changes the Brunnstrom approach is designed to elicit.

### "Against" position — Brunnstrom as therapy is superseded

**Named critics + position:**
- **Physiopedia 2023** notes explicitly: *"There are several methods on rehabilitation of stroke patients however very little evidence is present"* for Brunnstrom therapy.
- **Theoretical objection (Levin & Piscitelli 2022 [4]):** Brunnstrom's foundational reflex-hierarchical motor-control theory is contested at the motor-control-theory level. Using reflexes and synergies to drive recovery is mid-20th-century neurophysiology; current motor-learning literature does not support reflex-driven facilitation as causally separable from practice intensity.
- **Indirect SR evidence:** Kollen 2009 [10], Pollock 2014, Scrivener 2020 — none of these SRs of Bobath / facilitation-based approaches find consistent superiority over task-specific training. By extension (Brunnstrom is a facilitation-based approach), the general SR-level evidence does not support Brunnstrom therapy as a primary intervention.
- **No major contemporary SR specifically on Brunnstrom therapy as treatment** retrieved within web-cap. The thin-evidence gap is itself the position.

### "For" position — Brunnstrom Stages framework as staging tool

**Named defenders (validity literature):**
- **Fugl-Meyer Assessment validity** (Gladstone 2002, PMID 12234086) [12]: the FMA's seven-stage motor-recovery structure derives directly from the Brunnstrom Stages framework. Conclusion of the FMA review: *"the Fugl-Meyer motor scale is recommended highly as a clinical and research tool for evaluating changes in motor impairment."* The FMA is widely used internationally — i.e., the Brunnstrom Stages framework persists embedded in mainstream assessment tools even where Brunnstrom Therapy isn't named.
- **Hsieh et al. 2016** — explicitly working to "improve the utility of the Brunnstrom recovery stages" as a staging tool.

### Where the genuine evidence sits

- **Stages framework: alive and well.** Embedded in FMA, widely used. No serious challenge to its descriptive utility as a recovery-staging tool.
- **Brunnstrom Therapy as intervention: thin evidence + theoretical headwinds.** Pandian 2011 [11] is one small RCT showing BHM > MRP for chronic hand recovery — but generalisability + the broader SR-level pattern (facilitation-based approaches not categorically superior to task-specific) constrain interpretation.
- **The build's honest position:** the framework-vs-therapy distinction in Brunnstrom mirrors the framework-vs-treatment distinction in Bobath (Debate 1) — but it's *more clearly resolved in the evidence* for Brunnstrom (framework retained, therapy thin) than for Bobath (still actively contested).

### Vested-interest signal

- **Very low.** No commercial Brunnstrom-tutor infrastructure equivalent to Bobath / EBTA. Brunnstrom's name persists via the Stages framework and textbook ("Movement Therapy in Hemiplegia"), not via a credentialing body.

### UK-specific anchoring

- **No UK-specific Brunnstrom institution or training body** identified (consistent with the earlier schools-landscape finding).
- **NICE NG236 and ISWP / RCP 2023** [6, 8] do not name Brunnstrom Therapy as a recommended approach. Neither name it as discouraged either — guideline silence on it.

### Named gaps

- **Pandian 2011 [11] generalisability to UK NHS chronic stroke caseload** not established.
- **Replication studies of Pandian 2011** not retrieved within web-cap.
- **Cochrane SR specifically on Brunnstrom therapy as intervention** — does not appear to exist as of this pass. Absence-of-evidence vs evidence-of-absence: gap on the SR side itself.

## Debate 5 — Task-specific training vs eclectic mix

### The contested claim

*"Task-specific / repetitive task training (TST/RTT) is the recommended primary approach to stroke motor rehabilitation; named-approach eclecticism (Bobath + Carr-Shepherd + PNF + Brunnstrom mixed by clinician preference) is sub-optimal"* — vs — *"UK clinicians can and do cheerfully switch between named approaches based on patient presentation and clinical experience; the guideline-recommended interventions are one input among many."*

Dad's call (`user-profile-investigation.md` § B) surfaced the eclectic-mix observation: UK clinicians switch between schools based on patient.

### "For" position (TST/RTT as primary) — what UK + ESO 2023 guidelines actually say

**The killer artefact: Olaoye, Lokesh, Aboderin & Ojo 2024** (*Healthcare* 12: 1433, "Recommendations for Upper Limb Motor Recovery") [9]. Comparative analysis of NICE 2023 NG236 + ISWP (RCP) 2023 + European Stroke Organisation 2023 guidelines for upper-limb stroke rehabilitation.

**Three-guideline comparison (Table 2 in [9]):**

| Approach | NICE NG236 [6,7] | ISWP / RCP 2023 [8] | ESO 2023 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Therapeutic intensity | Offer min 3 hr therapy on ≥5 days/week | Same (min 3 hr × ≥5 days/week) | Strongly recommend as much as possible |
| Strength training | Consider | Offer | Strongly recommend |
| **Task-specific training / repetitive task training (TST/RTT)** | **Offer RTT/TST** | **Offer RTT/TST** | **Strongly recommend RTT/TST** |
| Mental practice | No recommendation | Offer as adjunct | Strongly recommend as adjunct |

**Key findings for the build [9]:**
- **All three guidelines AGREE** on TST/RTT. NICE "Offer", ISWP "Offer", ESO "Strongly recommend".
- **ISWP positions RTT specifically:** *"RTT should be the principal rehabilitation method alongside traditional exercise"* (load-bearing quote).
- **ISWP definition of RTT:** *"intensive, task-specific, functional and consisting of a high number of repetitions"*.
- **ESO definition of RTT:** *"intensive, task-specific, progressively adaptive, goal-oriented and meaningful"*.
- **NICE NG236 1.13.20:** *"Offer people after stroke repetitive task training on a range of activities for upper limb weakness (such as tasks that involve reaching, grasping, pointing, moving and manipulating objects) and lower limb weakness (such as sit-to-stand transfers, walking and using stairs)."* [7]
- **No named approaches (Bobath / Carr-Shepherd / PNF / Brunnstrom / Peto) appear in NICE NG236 recommendations** [6, 7]. Guideline language is intervention-named (CIMT, RTT, strength training, ES, mirror therapy), not approach-named.
- **ISWP / RCP 2023 [8]:** same intervention-named pattern.

This is the **guideline-level resolution** of Debate 5. The UK guideline bodies do not pick a winning *approach*; they pick winning *interventions* and instantiate them in approach-agnostic language. Eclectic-mix vs task-specific-only is partly a false dichotomy at the guideline level: the guidelines recommend specific intervention types (RTT, strength, CIMT for eligible) regardless of which approach the clinician identifies with.

**Supporting SR evidence [10]:**
- **Scrivener 2020:** task-specific training > Bobath therapy for lower-limb activities (SMD 0.48, 95% CI 0.01-0.95).
- **Pollock 2014:** Bobath not superior to other interventions; task-specific training preferable.
- **Kollen 2009** [10]: best-evidence synthesis verdict: *"no evidence is available for the superiority of any approach"* — but where direct comparisons exist (Platz Arm BASIS > Bobath; Thaut RAS > NDT/Bobath gait training), task-specific / structured interventions outperform Bobath.
- **Veerbeek et al. 2014** (referenced in [5] as ref 5): "the effectiveness of most interventions for the upper and lower limbs is driven by repetition and principles of task- and context-specific motor learning."

### "Against" position — eclectic-mix as clinically realistic

**Named voices:**
- **Tyson & Selley 2007** (UK survey, cited in [1]): UK therapists "seemed to follow a traditional Bobath model in practice, despite their self-perception of being eclectic." This actually undercuts the eclectic-mix defence — UK therapists *describe* themselves as eclectic but practice patterns reveal underlying tradition-lean.
- **Dad's call** (`user-profile-investigation.md` § B): UK clinicians "cheerfully switch between schools" — first-person UK clinical practice testimony.
- **Mayston 2008** [1] herself frames the move forward as not approach-based: *"an integrated approach to neurorehabilitation that is not based on approaches, but rather is client based with a sound theoretical, and where possible, evidence base."* I.e., Mayston is **against** approach-based eclecticism and **for** intervention-based individualisation.
- **ISWP / RCP 2023 [8]** language: "Personalised rehabilitation types and intensity are also recommended by NICE and ISWP, which should be offered by a range of multidisciplinary team members" — i.e., guideline-recommended personalisation, not eclectic-school-mixing.

**The actual nuance the build must surface:** clinical practice IS often eclectic, but the *guideline-recommended* version of eclectic is **intervention-level personalisation grounded in RTT + strength + CIMT-when-eligible + appropriate adjuncts**, not **named-approach switching between Bobath / Carr-Shepherd / Brunnstrom on clinical preference**. The two look similar in practice but are theoretically distinct positions.

### Where the genuine evidence sits

- **At the guideline level (NICE NG236 [6,7] + ISWP/RCP 2023 [8] + ESO 2023 [9]):** TST/RTT is universally recommended as the principal rehabilitation method. Named approaches (Bobath / Carr-Shepherd / PNF / Brunnstrom) are not endorsed by name in current UK + European guidelines.
- **At the SR level:** task-specific training outperforms Bobath in direct comparisons (Pollock 2014, Scrivener 2020, Platz trial in [10]). No SR endorses approach-switching as a clinical strategy.
- **At the practice-pattern level:** UK clinicians describe themselves as eclectic but practice patterns show tradition-lean (Tyson & Selley 2007 [1]). The eclectic-mix description may be more cultural than empirically grounded.
- **At the motor-control theory level [4]:** there is no unified motor-control theory to ground eclectic-approach-mixing. The theoretical headwinds favour intervention-based, not approach-based, practice.

### Vested-interest signal

- **Guideline bodies (NICE, ISWP, ESO) have institutional incentive to consolidate around evidence-based interventions** — this is their job, not a hidden vested interest. The consolidation toward TST/RTT is the *output* of the guideline process, not a bias in the process.
- **Bobath / PNF / Brunnstrom credential bodies** have vested interest in the eclectic-mix framing because it preserves named-approach courses as legitimate CPD content.

### UK-specific anchoring

- **NICE NG236 §1.13.20 (Repetitive task training)** [6, 7]: load-bearing UK source.
- **ISWP / National Clinical Guideline for Stroke 2023** [8]: load-bearing UK source.
- **Olaoye et al. 2024** [9]: UK-affiliated review explicitly comparing the three guideline bodies.
- **Tyson & Selley 2007** [1]: UK practice-pattern survey.
- **Dad's call** (`user-profile-investigation.md` § B): contemporary UK clinical voice naming the eclectic-mix pattern.

### Named gaps

- **2024-2026 ACPIN / CSP CPD course catalogues** — do UK CPD providers teach task-specific training as the primary content vs continuing to teach named approaches? Not retrieved.
- **Implementation-research evidence on what UK NHS stroke teams actually deliver** — do they hit the NICE-recommended 3 hr/day × ≥5 days/week, with TST/RTT as principal method? Not retrieved.
- **Comparative effectiveness of "eclectic-mix" as a defined intervention vs guideline-recommended RTT** — no SR appears to exist on this; it would be hard to define eclectic-mix rigorously for trial inclusion.

## Cross-debate synthesis

The 5 debates share a common structural pattern the build's debate-mode AI should surface explicitly:

1. **The "for" position rests on either institutional defence + theoretical/historical claim OR small-trial empirical evidence.**
2. **The "against" position rests on SR/meta-analytic evidence + current guideline recommendations + foundational motor-control theory critique.**
3. **Where the genuine evidence sits** is usually **not** at the level the debate is framed at — the named-approaches debate is over-determined at the SR level (task-specific wins where directly compared) but the framework-vs-method distinction (Debate 1) is theoretical / non-empirical and not refuted by the SR evidence.
4. **Vested-interest signals are real and named** for Bobath > PNF > CIMT > Brunnstrom ≈ Peto, in roughly that order.
5. **UK guideline bodies (NICE NG236, RCP/ISWP 2023) have already consolidated** around intervention-named recommendations (TST/RTT, strength, CIMT for eligible, ES, mirror therapy) — approach-named language (Bobath / Brunnstrom) has been deliberately removed from current UK guidelines.

The AI build's debate-mode is most differentiated when it surfaces:
- **The asymmetry of evidence cited by "for" and "against" positions** (institutional/theoretical vs SR/guideline).
- **The intervention-vs-approach distinction** that current UK guidelines embody.
- **The named gaps** where contested claims are not actually empirically resolved (especially Debate 1's framework-vs-treatment distinction and Debate 2's facilitation-as-mechanism question).
